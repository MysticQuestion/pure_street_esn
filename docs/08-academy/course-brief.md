STREETS ACADEMY

Oakland Waste & Recycling Certification

Flagship Course

Waste Literacy, Resource Recovery & Clean Streets

Course designation: STREETS-WR101
Format: Interactive online certification + optional field practicum
Core course length: Approximately 60–90 minutes
Field practicum: Optional 60–120 minutes
Credential: OaklandSTREETS Waste & Recycling Literacy Certificate
Certificate validity: 2 years, with short annual update modules recommended

---

COURSE PURPOSE

Waste is not simply something that disappears after collection.

Every discarded object enters a physical, economic, regulatory, environmental, and logistical system.

Understanding that system is the purpose of STREETS Academy.

Participants learn to:

- identify Oakland's major waste streams;
- correctly distinguish recycling, compost, trash, hazardous waste, bulky waste, and specialty materials;
- recognize contamination;
- understand illegal dumping and its prevention;
- understand the roles of residents, businesses, property owners, haulers, local government, and recycling organizations;
- recognize environmental and public-health consequences of improper disposal;
- conduct basic waste-condition observations;
- communicate recycling information accurately;
- know when an issue belongs with WM, California Waste Solutions, Oakland 311, StopWaste, a property owner, or another service;
- understand the fundamentals of California organics-diversion requirements;
- document conditions without misrepresenting responsibility or causation.

The course treats recycling literacy as civic infrastructure.

MODULE 1

Where Does "Away" Actually Go?

Essential question

What happens after something leaves your hand?

Learning objectives

Participants understand:

- the difference between collection, sorting, processing, composting, recycling, reuse, and disposal;
- why incorrectly sorted material can compromise otherwise recoverable material;
- why waste systems depend on both infrastructure and human behavior;
- why waste prevention sits above disposal.

Core lesson

Throwing something away is not the end of its physical existence.

Every object requires another decision.

It may be:

reused;

repaired;

recycled into another material;

processed as organic matter;

handled through a specialized waste stream;

or buried as landfill waste.

Recycling therefore begins before collection.

The first sorting decision often occurs in someone's kitchen, workplace, apartment building, restaurant, school, or street.

Interaction

Trace the object

Participant follows four objects through possible disposal pathways:

- aluminum can;
- banana peel;
- plastic bag;
- lithium battery.

The system demonstrates how one incorrect sorting decision changes the object's pathway.

Knowledge check

5 questions.

MODULE 3

Compost Is Not "Anything Biodegradable"

This module should deliberately correct one of the largest public misconceptions.

Oakland compost accepts:

Food

- fruits;
- vegetables;
- meat;
- bones;
- dairy;
- eggs;
- beans;
- grains.

Food-soiled paper

- napkins;
- paper towels;
- greasy pizza boxes;
- qualifying uncoated fiber containers;
- coffee filters;
- paper tea bags.

Plant debris

- leaves;
- grass;
- branches;
- flowers.

Certain untreated wood items

- wooden stirrers;
- chopsticks;
- similar untreated wood.

Oakland compost does NOT accept:

- plastic bags;
- so-called compostable plastic bags;
- PLA and other compostable plastic foodware;
- plastic-lined cups;
- diapers;
- pet waste;
- Styrofoam;
- bottles;
- cans;
- glass;
- hazardous waste.

Critical lesson

A label saying "compostable" does not automatically mean an item belongs in Oakland's compost collection system.

MODULE 4

Trash: The Residual Stream

Trash should be taught as the remaining disposal category, not the default category.

Typical Oakland trash includes:

- plastic bags;
- plastic film;
- bubble wrap;
- disposable plastic utensils;
- straws;
- Styrofoam;
- diapers;
- pet waste;
- broken ceramics;
- chip bags;
- candy wrappers;
- many mixed-material packages;
- compostable plastic/PLA foodware;
- hoses, wires, and similar tanglers.

Principle

Before choosing trash, ask:

1. Can this be reused?
2. Can this be recycled?
3. Can this be composted?
4. Does this require specialized disposal?

If all four answers are no, trash may be appropriate.

Exercise

Last Chance Before Landfill

15 difficult objects.

MODULE 5

Dangerous, Special & Misplaced Materials

Some materials should never be casually placed in any standard collection container.

Introduce categories such as:

- batteries;
- paint;
- household chemicals;
- fluorescent lamps;
- electronics;
- sharps;
- automotive fluids;
- propane cylinders;
- certain pesticides;
- other household hazardous wastes.

Core rule

When uncertain about hazardous material, do not improvise.

Use an authorized disposal resource.

Interactive component

Red Flag

Learners receive a street scene containing ordinary waste and potentially hazardous material.

They identify which objects require escalation rather than handling.

Safety competency

STREETS trainees are taught:

observe → document → maintain distance → refer

rather than:

touch → open → investigate

MODULE 6

Bulky Waste & Illegal Dumping

Essential distinction

A mattress on the sidewalk is not merely "trash."

It represents a service pathway problem.

Participants learn:

- what bulky waste is;
- the difference between legitimate scheduled bulky collection and illegal dumping;
- why dumping next to a dumpster does not make the material part of normal collection;
- why waste beside a street litter receptacle is not automatically legitimate disposal;
- how Oakland residents can access existing bulky-service resources;
- when OAK 311 is the appropriate reporting channel.

Scenario

A sofa appears beside an apartment building.

Ask:

What do you actually know?

A. The tenant dumped it illegally.
B. WM failed to collect it.
C. The landlord abandoned it.
D. A bulky item is present and its origin and service status remain unverified.

Correct answer:

D.

This teaches one of STREETS' most important principles:

DOCUMENT CONDITIONS, NOT ASSUMPTIONS.

MODULE 7

Who Does What in Oakland?

This should be a signature lesson.

Participants frequently assume one company or one City department controls the entire waste system.

They do not.

System map

Waste Management of Alameda County

Among its Oakland responsibilities are trash and compost collection, alongside other applicable services.

California Waste Solutions

Provides residential recycling collection in Oakland.

City of Oakland

Administers municipal requirements and services including illegal-dumping response pathways.

StopWaste

The Alameda County Waste Management Authority provides countywide waste-reduction programs and plays an important role in organics-recovery implementation and enforcement.

Property owners and businesses

Have their own responsibilities concerning adequate service, sorting, education, container access, and property conditions.

Residents

Are participants in the system, not merely customers at the end of it.

Interaction

Route the Case

10 incidents.

Learner chooses the most appropriate first pathway.

MODULE 8

Contamination: When the Right Bin Goes Wrong

Participants learn that recycling performance is not simply about how much material enters a recycling container.

It is also about quality.

Contamination examples

Plastic bag inside recycling.

Food inside recycling.

Glass bottle inside compost.

PLA cup inside compost.

Bagged compostables inside a plastic bag.

Diapers inside compost.

Field classification

STREETS can use:

Level 0 — Clean

No obvious contamination.

Level 1 — Minor

Isolated incorrect items.

Level 2 — Moderate

Repeated visible contamination.

Level 3 — Severe

Contamination materially dominates or compromises the observable load.

Important limitation

The course makes clear:

STREETS field observations are not official hauler contamination determinations unless made under an authorized partnership.

MODULE 9

SB 1383 Without the Bureaucratic Fog

Why organic waste matters

Organic material decomposing in landfill conditions generates methane.

California's SB 1383 framework is designed in significant part to reduce organic material entering landfills.

Oakland business and multifamily responsibilities

Training explains, in plain language, applicable duties including:

- recycling and compost service;
- properly situated and labeled indoor containers;
- proper sorting;
- recurring education;
- inspection of containers;
- feedback when materials are sorted improperly;
- applicable tenant education;
- edible-food recovery obligations for covered food-generating entities.

Business track

Course detects account type:

Resident

STREETS field trainee

and adapts this module accordingly.

MODULE 10

Waste Prevention Comes Before Recycling

Flagship educational programs should not inadvertently teach consumerism followed by sorting.

Introduce the hierarchy:

REFUSE

Avoid unnecessary material.

REDUCE

Consume less material.

REUSE

Keep material functioning.

REPAIR

Extend product life.

SHARE / BORROW / RENT

Avoid duplicative ownership.

DONATE / REDISTRIBUTE

Preserve utility.

RECYCLE / COMPOST

Recover material.

DISPOSE

Use landfill only for the residual waste stream.

Challenge

Participant receives a household move-out scenario and must minimize landfill disposal.

MODULE 11

Clean Streets as Environmental Justice

Waste burdens are geographically unequal.

Participants examine how persistent dumping can affect:

- pedestrian access;
- disabled residents;
- children;
- transit riders;
- local businesses;
- stormwater systems;
- neighborhood aesthetics;
- public health;
- perceptions of public investment.

The lesson avoids reducing environmental justice to symbolism.

Participants instead investigate measurable questions:

Where do conditions recur?

How long do they remain?

Which neighborhoods experience repeated exposure?

Which intervention actually reduces recurrence?

Who has access to legitimate disposal options?

MODULE 12

The STREETS Field Method

This transforms ordinary graduates into potential citizen observers.

STREETS OBSERVATION PROTOCOL

For every condition:

1. Observe

What is physically present?

2. Locate

Record sufficiently precise location information.

3. Classify

Identify condition type.

4. Assess

Estimate severity, volume, obstruction, and possible hazards.

5. Document

Photograph where lawful and appropriate.

6. Separate fact from inference

Never assign blame without evidence.

7. Route

Determine the appropriate service pathway.

8. Follow

Check whether the condition changes.

9. Close

Document verified resolution where possible.

10. Analyze

Ask whether the condition recurs.

STREETS evidentiary rule

See what is there. Record what is known. Label what remains uncertain.

STREETS ACADEMY STANDARD

A graduate should leave the course capable of answering four questions reliably:

WHAT IS IT?

Identify the material or condition.

WHERE DOES IT GO?

Determine the correct waste or service pathway.

WHO HANDLES IT?

Understand institutional responsibility.

WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?

Take an accurate, safe, documented action.

That is the baseline standard for OaklandSTREETS waste literacy.
